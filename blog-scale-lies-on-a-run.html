Why the scale lies during a run of dates

Anthony · 4 min read

Every coach gets this message. Somebody four days into a run texts me a number, convinced everything has come off the rails. It almost never has.

Here's what's actually moving.

Fat doesn't move that fast

Start here, because it settles most of the panic. Body fat changes slowly. It's a matter of energy balance over weeks, and it cannot swing several pounds in either direction over a weekend. The arithmetic doesn't allow it.

So when the number jumps four pounds between Thursday and Sunday, whatever moved, it wasn't fat. The scale isn't measuring fat. It's measuring everything — water, food in transit, glycogen, inflammation — and on a run of dates, every single one of those is in motion at once.

What's actually going on during a run

Four things stack up, and they all push the same direction.

  • Sodium. Restaurant and gas station food carries far more of it than anything you'd cook. Sodium pulls water with it, and that water shows up on the scale within a day.
  • Training and bump damage. Hard work and hard landings cause inflammation, and inflammation means fluid retention in the tissue that's repairing. You are, briefly, holding water in the places you worked hardest.
  • Sleep debt. Short, broken sleep in a strange room raises cortisol, and elevated cortisol holds onto water. This alone can account for a couple of pounds by the end of a run.
  • Timing. You're weighing at different hours, on different scales, after different meals. Hotel bathroom scales aren't calibrated and no two agree.

Four inputs, all pointing up, none of them fat.

The scale during a run isn't telling you about your progress. It's telling you that you've been travelling, which you already knew.

What to actually measure

Weigh yourself, but change what you do with the number.

Use the weekly average, not the daily reading. Weigh at the same time each morning, write it down, and only compare this week's average against last week's. The daily numbers are noise. The averages are signal. Most people who feel like they're failing are looking at noise.

Watch the trend over three weeks. One week of averages still isn't much. Three weeks tells you whether you're actually moving.

Use the mirror and photos on the same schedule. Same spot, same light, same time of day, same poses. Photos two weeks apart tell you more than fourteen scale readings.

Watch performance. If your working weights are holding while you're in a deficit, you're keeping muscle and losing what you meant to lose. That's the actual goal, and the scale can't see it.

Where it lands

Give it three or four days after you're home. Sleep normalises, sodium normalises, inflammation goes down, and the number falls back to where it really was. Every time.

The people who struggle with this aren't the ones whose weight fluctuates. Everyone's weight fluctuates. They're the ones who react to the fluctuation — who slash calories mid-run because of a number that was never fat in the first place, and then spend the following week too depleted to train properly.

Don't do that. Log the number, ignore the number, look at the week.
