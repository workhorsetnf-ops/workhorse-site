How to eat on the road without guessing

Anthony · 5 min read

The plan that falls apart on the road isn't a bad plan. It's a plan written by someone who assumed you'd have a kitchen.

Most nutrition advice quietly depends on things you don't have: a fridge, a scale, two hours on Sunday, and knowing on Tuesday where you'll be Thursday. Take those away and the plan doesn't bend. It breaks, and then you decide you have no discipline, which isn't what happened at all.

Here's what I actually do, and what I give clients who are living out of a car.

Anchor the protein, let everything else float

If you try to hold calories, protein, carbs and fat all in place on a travel day, you'll miss all four. Pick one to defend.

Make it protein. It's the thing that protects the muscle you're working to keep, it's the hardest to accidentally hit, and it's the one that keeps you full enough that the rest of the day doesn't get away from you. Carbs and fat can land wherever they land on a bad day. If the protein hit, the day was fine.

This isn't a compromise. It's the actual priority order, and knowing it means you're not negotiating with yourself at a gas station at eleven at night.

Build a short list and stop re-deciding

Decision fatigue is the real enemy on the road, not the food options. By the fourth town you don't want to evaluate a menu, so you eat whatever's fastest.

So take the deciding out. Have five or six default orders you already know work, and order them without looking. Mine come from the places that actually exist off an interstate at odd hours:

  • Rotisserie chicken from a grocery store, eaten out of the container. Nearly every town has one, and it's the single most reliable protein on the road.
  • Two or three grilled chicken sandwiches from a fast food place, bread on or off depending on the day.
  • A burrito bowl — double meat, skip the parts that turn it into a thousand calories you didn't taste.
  • Deli counter turkey and a bag of pre-washed greens. Ninety seconds in any supermarket.
  • Greek yogurt and fruit for the meal you'd otherwise skip.

Six options is enough. The goal isn't variety, it's not thinking.

Carry the gap-fillers

There will be a stretch — a drive, a delay, a building with nothing but vending — where the choice is a bad option or nothing. That's what the bag is for.

Protein powder in a shaker, a couple of bars you've actually checked the label on, and beef jerky covers most of it. This isn't your nutrition plan. It's the thing that stops a five-hour gap from turning into a two-thousand-calorie decision at midnight.

Nobody ever got out of shape because of the meal they ate. They got out of shape because of the meal they didn't, and what they did four hours later.

Hotel breakfast is a real meal, use it

It's free, it's protein, and you're already awake. Eggs, whatever meat is there, yogurt if they have it. Skip the waffle machine and the pastry tray, not on moral grounds, but because they'll leave you hungry by ten and you won't have options at ten.

Getting a solid meal in before you leave the hotel takes the pressure off the whole rest of the day. That's the entire trick.

Track the week, not the day

A travel day will be off. Some days you'll be low, some you'll be over. That's normal and it isn't the problem.

What matters is the seven-day average, because that's the timescale your body actually responds to. One bad day inside a good week does nothing. The damage comes from writing off the week because Tuesday went sideways — which is a decision, not a consequence.

The part nobody tells you

This gets easier, but not because your willpower improves. It gets easier because the decisions get made in advance, once, instead of forty times a month while you're tired.

Build the list. Pack the bag. Defend the protein. Let the rest be imperfect.
